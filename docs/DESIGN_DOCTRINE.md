# Design Doctrine

## Purpose

The portfolio exists so a visitor can quickly understand:

- who Kai is;
- what he does;
- what he is doing now;
- what he has built;
- where he has worked;
- what kind of person he is; and
- how to contact him.

The interface is subordinate to this information. A visual decision is successful when it helps a visitor answer one or more of these questions with less effort.

## Design philosophy

The design is informed by Dieter Rams. “Less” means removing interference, not removing character.

Every meaningful element should serve at least one of these purposes:

- understanding;
- hierarchy;
- navigation;
- evidence;
- interaction; or
- identity.

If an element serves none of them, question why it exists. Order, consistency, and legible relationships matter more than novelty.

## Information density

Do not equate whitespace with quality. Whitespace should establish grouping, hierarchy, or breathing room; it should not exist merely to make the site feel premium.

Related information should be physically close. Visitors should not have to move their eyes or scroll unnecessarily to connect information that belongs together. Dense presentation is acceptable when hierarchy and grouping remain clear. Never reduce text readability merely to increase density.

## Identity

Kai's identity comes from a small number of deliberate elements:

- handmade or pixel artwork;
- direct, human writing;
- unusual and personal project motivations; and
- the restrained cat-bat illustration on the homepage.

Do not keep adding motifs merely to make the site look designed. Identity should emerge from a coherent system, not accumulated decoration.

## Typography

Use a readable sans-serif throughout. Typography should establish hierarchy without becoming the subject of the page. The approved hierarchy is deliberately modest: compact navigation, a small page heading, readable body copy, and understated metadata.

## Honesty

Never invent:

- screenshots;
- metrics;
- interfaces;
- project maturity;
- responsibilities; or
- outcomes.

An unfinished project may appear unfinished. Presenting its real concept clearly is better than implying progress or polish that does not exist.

## Personality

Personality should come from real details: missing Overwatch queues while getting food, handmade hamster or spider artwork, or what Kai is currently reading, playing, learning, and building.

Do not manufacture personality with generic clever section copy. Direct writing and specific lived details are more memorable than slogans.

## No trend imitation

Do not introduce patterns simply because they are common in modern portfolios. Avoid these unless a specific functional need clearly justifies them:

- bento grids;
- giant editorial headings;
- huge empty hero areas;
- glassmorphism;
- gradients;
- fake terminals;
- giant project numbering;
- decorative status pills;
- scrolling marquees; and
- excessive rounded cards.

## Design test

For every significant element, ask:

1. What job does this perform?
2. Does it make information easier to understand?
3. Is it competing with something more important?
4. Is it honest?
5. Is it part of Kai's identity or generic decoration?
6. Could it be removed without losing meaning?
7. Are its spacing, alignment, typography, and interaction deliberate?

If these questions do not produce a clear reason for the element, remove or simplify it.
